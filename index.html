Abstract

Background: Chronic diseases including diabetes, obesity, and kidney disease represent a leading burden on the U.S. healthcare system, with lifestyle behaviors serving as primary modifiable risk factors.1,2 Multi-year surveillance data at population scale enables detection of trends that cross-sectional studies cannot capture.

Methods: We analyzed 3,482,496 survey records from the CDC Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) 2017–2024 using Apache PySpark distributed computing. Logistic regression and random forest classifiers were trained to predict diabetes risk from lifestyle and demographic features.

Results: Diabetes prevalence increased from 14.2% (2017) to 15.2% (2024); obesity rose from 31.1% to 33.7%. Obese adults showed a 23.5% diabetes rate versus 7.3% in normal-weight adults. A clear lifestyle dose-response was observed: adults with lifestyle score 4/4 showed 6.6% diabetes prevalence vs. 18.6% for score 0/4. Best model AUC = 0.797.

Conclusions: Physical activity, BMI, and general health self-rating are the strongest modifiable predictors of diabetes risk. These findings support integrated lifestyle screening approaches applicable to large-scale administrative databases such as CMS Medicare claims.

Section 1

Background & Research Questions

Chronic diseases account for 90% of the $4.1 trillion in annual U.S. healthcare expenditures, with diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease representing disproportionate shares.1 The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), conducted annually by the CDC since 1984, provides the nation's largest telephone-based health surveillance dataset — covering all 50 states plus D.C. and U.S. territories, with approximately 400,000–460,000 respondents per year.3

Despite the scale of BRFSS, multi-year analyses pooling 3+ years of data remain relatively uncommon, partly because the full cross-decade dataset exceeds practical limits for single-machine processing tools such as pandas. Apache PySpark's distributed architecture addresses this bottleneck, enabling population-scale analysis at the granularity required for detecting modest but meaningful longitudinal trends.4

This analysis addresses three research questions:

  • How have diabetes, obesity, and physical activity prevalence changed across the U.S. adult population from 2017 to 2024?
  • Which lifestyle factors — physical activity, BMI, sleep, and mental health — are most strongly associated with diabetes risk after adjustment for demographics?
  • How accurately can machine learning models classify diabetes risk from BRFSS lifestyle and demographic variables, and what is the relative contribution of modifiable vs. non-modifiable predictors?
Section 2

Data & Methods

2.1 Data source

Data were obtained from the CDC BRFSS annual public-use files for survey years 2017–2024 (files: LLCP20XX.XPT, SAS Transport format).3 BRFSS collects state-specific data on health risk behaviors, chronic diseases, and preventive health service use from the non-institutionalized adult population (≥18 years) via telephone interview. Since 2011, BRFSS has employed a dual-frame design combining landline and cellular telephone sampling, with iterative proportional fitting (raking) weights to improve representativeness across demographic margins.5

2.2 Variable harmonization

Several core variables changed names across survey years due to BRFSS rotating-core design, in which some questions alternate between even and odd years. Blood pressure (hypertension) was collected only in odd years (2017, 2019, 2021, 2023) using variables BPHIGH4/BPHIGH6; sleep duration was collected only in even years plus 2017. A systematic alias-resolution layer was implemented in the PySpark pipeline to map canonical variable names to their year-specific equivalents, with NaN-fill for years where a question was not administered. BRFSS-standard missing value codes (7, 9, 77, 99, 777, 999) were replaced with null; Spark float NaN values (introduced by the pandas bridge layer) were handled separately via F.isnan().

2.3 Why PySpark

At 3.5 million records and 25+ variables per year, loading the full multi-year file into a single machine's RAM using pandas requires ~8GB and degrades processing speed substantially. PySpark partitions data across multiple CPU cores locally (or cluster nodes in production), enabling the same analytical pipeline to scale from a laptop to a cloud cluster — the architecture used in production healthcare data systems such as CMS Medicare claims processing.4 The identical code runs without modification on Databricks or AWS EMR for true distributed deployment.

2.4 Analytical pipeline

The pipeline comprises six modular classes: BRFSSLoader → BRFSSCleaner → BRFSSEngineer → BRFSSAnalyzer → BRFSSModeler → BRFSSPipeline. All functions include Google-style docstrings and Python logging (replacing print statements) to enable production-grade log capture. Output is saved in Apache Parquet format for efficient downstream analysis. An 80/20 train-test split was used with fixed seed (20260301) for reproducibility.

Sample Characteristics

The combined analytic dataset includes 3,482,496 adult respondents across 8 survey years (2017–2024). Mean age was 55.0 years; mean BMI was 28.4 kg/m²; mean nightly sleep was 6.9 hours (years with available data). Annual sample sizes ranged from 401,958 (2020, partially affected by COVID-19 field operations) to 457,670 (2024).

Key Findings

4.1 Rising diabetes and obesity (2017–2024)

Finding 1

Diabetes prevalence increased from 14.2% (2017) to 15.2% (2024) — a 1.0 percentage-point rise over 8 years. Obesity rose from 31.1% to 33.7% (+2.6pp). Physical activity improved modestly from 72.4% to 76.7% reporting any activity.

4.2 BMI and diabetes — strong dose-response

Finding 2

Diabetes prevalence shows a clear BMI dose-response: 23.5% in obese adults vs. 7.3% in normal-weight adults — a 3.2-fold difference. Underweight adults show the lowest diabetes prevalence (6.2%), consistent with type 1 diabetes predominance in this subgroup.6

4.3 Age gradient in chronic disease

Finding 3

Diabetes risk rises sharply with age: 2.2% in 18–29-year-olds to 21.4% in adults 75+. CKD shows the steepest age gradient, rising from 0.7% to 8.0% — a more than 11-fold increase. Physical activity declines with age, from 82.6% to 65.8%.7

4.4 Lifestyle score — clear dose-response

Finding 4

A composite healthy lifestyle score (0–4: physical activity + adequate sleep + healthy BMI + non-smoking) shows a robust dose-response relationship with diabetes prevalence. Adults scoring 4/4 show 6.6% diabetes prevalence vs. 18.6% for score 0/4 — a 2.8-fold difference — after combining across all 8 survey years.

Section 5

Machine Learning: Diabetes Risk Classification

5.1 Model performance

Two models were trained on 2,517,825 records (80%) and evaluated on 629,712 records (20%). The training dataset used only records with non-null values for the outcome (diabetes flag) and core demographic features (age, BMI, sex), allowing optional features (sleep, mental health days) to be missing without exclusion — appropriate for the rotating-core survey design. Feature vectors with null values were handled via handleInvalid="skip" in VectorAssembler.

5.2 Feature importances

General health self-rating — a single-item 5-point scale (Excellent to Poor) — is by far the strongest predictor (importance 0.348), consistent with published evidence that self-rated health captures latent health status beyond what objective biomarkers alone can explain.8 Age and BMI follow, with physical activity (pa_any) and the composite lifestyle score retaining independent predictive value after clinical adjustment.

Section 6

Discussion & Conclusions

This analysis of 3.5 million BRFSS respondents across 8 survey years confirms persistent and worsening trends in diabetes (14.2%→15.2%) and obesity (31.1%→33.7%) in the U.S. adult population — findings consistent with national surveillance estimates from CDC and the National Diabetes Statistics Report.2,6 The modest improvement in physical activity prevalence (72.4%→76.7%) did not offset the rising trajectory of metabolic disease.

The BMI-diabetes dose-response (7.3% in normal-weight vs. 23.5% in obese adults) aligns with extensive epidemiological literature establishing obesity as the primary modifiable driver of type 2 diabetes risk.9 The lifestyle score analysis — showing a 2.8-fold gradient from score 0 to score 4 — reinforces the primacy of multi-domain behavioral intervention over any single lifestyle factor.10

The strong predictive contribution of self-rated health (importance 0.348) is notable and consistent with meta-analytic evidence that single-item global health ratings are robust predictors of mortality, hospitalization, and incident chronic disease, capturing latent health state variance not accounted for by objective biomarkers alone.8

From a methodological perspective, the PySpark pipeline developed here — with modular class architecture, comprehensive docstrings, and Python logging — provides a scalable framework directly applicable to large administrative healthcare databases. The same pipeline could process CMS Medicare claims data (3M+ ESRD beneficiaries in KECC's database) without modification, simply by replacing the BRFSS XPT loader with a claims file reader.

Future directions. Longitudinal analysis linking individual respondents across years would strengthen causal inference. Survey-weighted estimates (using _LLCPWT) would enable population-representative projections. Integration with geographic data (Census tract-level SDOH indicators) via Spark spatial joins would add an environmental dimension to lifestyle-disease associations.11

Limitations

Limitations

  • Self-reported data. All BRFSS responses are self-reported, introducing social desirability bias — particularly for physical activity, BMI (height/weight), and disease diagnoses. Validated clinical measurements would strengthen findings.
  • Rotating-core design. Hypertension data is available only in odd survey years; sleep in 2017/2018/2020/2022. Year-over-year comparisons for these variables must account for structural missingness, not random attrition.
  • Cross-sectional structure. BRFSS is a repeated cross-section, not a longitudinal cohort. Causal inference about lifestyle-disease relationships requires longitudinal data with temporal ordering of exposures and outcomes.
  • Survey weighting not applied. Analyses use unweighted counts and proportions. Application of _LLCPWT raking weights is required for nationally representative population estimates — a planned extension.
  • Local computing. The pipeline ran in PySpark local mode on a single workstation. Production-scale deployment on a distributed cluster (Databricks, AWS EMR) would be required for real-time or streaming analytics on Medicare-scale datasets.
